How to spot a fake sneaker release ad or raffle

By the RestockRally desk

The FTC April 2026 spotlight says shopping was the most reported social media scam in 2025. What to check before a sneaker ad.

Last updated November 20, 2026

The short answer: if a sneaker ad or a "you won" message is where you first saw the pair, leave it and open the retailer's own site or app yourself. In an April 2026 Data Spotlight, the Federal Trade Commission said shopping was the most reported social media scam in 2025, and that more than 40 percent of people who lost money to a scam on social media said it started when they ordered something they had seen in an ad.

What the April 2026 spotlight counted

The spotlight covers reports to the FTC's Consumer Sentinel Network. It says that in 2025, nearly 30 percent of people who reported losing money said the contact started on social media, and that those reports added up to $2.1 billion. A footnote splits loss reports that named a contact method: website or app 31 percent, social media 28 percent, phone call 11 percent, email 10 percent, and text 7 percent. The same note says the FTC did not collect reports during the 2025 government shutdown, so the year is not a complete log. The body's "nearly 30 percent" and the footnote's 28 percent are the same pile of reports, counted two ways.

The footnote's dollar series, for reports that named social media as the contact method, runs $261 million in 2020, $789 million in 2021, $1.2 billion in 2022, $1.5 billion in 2023, $1.9 billion in 2024, and $2.1 billion in 2025. The spotlight calls the 2025 total about eight times the 2020 figure. It also says most of these losses are never reported, so the real total is likely higher. Read the PDF itself for the footnotes. We checked that file on 20 November 2026.

Shopping reports, and where the money actually went

Shopping was the most reported social media scam in that spotlight. It was not where most of the money went. Investment scams accounted for $1.1 billion, more than half the dollars reported lost on social media. The shopping reports are the ones that look like a release post: an ad, a familiar brand, a price that looks too low, then an order that never arrives or arrives very different from what the ad showed. The FTC says many of those ads led to unfamiliar websites, and others to sites impersonating well known brands with big discounts. It also says the goods in those reports were often shipped from China, with return shipping high enough that sending the item back did not make sense.

A limited sneaker creates the same hurry. Compare an ad with a page that names a date, a price, and a style code from the brand or the retailer. Today that means the G-Unit G6 restock, which Reebok lists at $130, and the Air Jordan 11 Space Jam return on 12 December. The Whitaker Group Campus 00s went up on adidas's own site. If the ad's price or date does not match the brand page, stop.

The check the spotlight actually gives you

Before you buy from an ad, the spotlight says to ask whether the company really offers that deal and whether the company is real, then search the name plus the words "scam" or "complaint." It tells you to report what you find at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Our rule on this site is stricter than that one search. A raffle is listed on the raffles page only after we confirm it on the retailer's own page. How online sneaker raffles work sets out what END., Foot Locker, and adidas say on their own help pages. For Nike's app, read how SNKRS Draw and Line launches work. An entry fee is a reason to stop and go to the retailer directly. That line is our rule, not a sentence from the spotlight. The consumer.ftc.gov pages this guide used to cite no longer resolve.
