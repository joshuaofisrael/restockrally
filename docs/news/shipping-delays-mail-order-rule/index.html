Sneaker Shipping Delays: What the FTC Mail Order Rule Requires

By the RestockRally desk

Understand shipping promises, delay notices, cancellation rights and refunds under the FTC Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule for online sneaker orders.

Last updated November 11, 2026 at 8:40 AM ET

A delayed sneaker shipment is not automatically a violation of federal law. The key questions are what the seller promised about shipping, whether it had a reasonable basis for that promise, and what it did once it learned the order could not ship on time. The Federal Trade Commission’s Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule, codified at 16 CFR Part 435, addresses those questions for covered orders placed online, by mail, or by telephone. (ecfr.gov)

The shipping deadline is not always 30 days

Under the FTC rule, a seller must have a reasonable basis to expect shipment within the time it clearly and conspicuously states. If it gives no shipment time, the usual default is 30 days after receiving a properly completed order. That is not a blanket promise that every online sneaker purchase must arrive within 30 days. The rule concerns shipment, not delivery at the customer's door. Shipment means placing merchandise physically in the carrier's possession.

Suppose a shoe listing says “ships within five business days.” The seller needs a reasonable basis for that five-day representation. It cannot simply rely on the general 30-day default instead. Conversely, if an eligible order contains no shipping representation, the default period generally applies. A special 50-day default can apply when a customer applies to the seller for credit to pay for the merchandise when ordering; that is not a universal extension for credit-card purchases.

A delivery estimate is also worth distinguishing from a shipping promise. Nike's shipping guidance says it displays estimated delivery dates at checkout and sends tracking information when an order ships. An estimated arrival date and the FTC's shipment obligation answer different questions. Save both the checkout estimate and the seller's specific shipping language.

What the seller must do when it cannot ship

If the merchant learns that it cannot meet the applicable shipping deadline, it must offer the buyer a choice: consent to a delay or cancel for a prompt refund. The notice must arrive within the original promised shipping period, or within the applicable default period if no time was specified. A seller may instead cancel and refund the order, notifying the buyer as required.

The first delay notice generally must give a revised shipping date or say that no definite date can be provided. It must explain the cancellation-and-refund option and provide a way to exercise it at the seller's expense. When the revised date is indefinite, the notice must explain the reason for the delay and the buyer's continuing right to cancel before shipment after consenting to that indefinite delay.

The FTC distinguishes shorter and longer first extensions. For a definite revised shipping date no more than 30 days beyond the original deadline, the seller may treat silence as consent only if the notice correctly explains that consequence and meets the rule's conditions. For a longer or indefinite extension, silence generally cannot simply be treated as an open-ended agreement: the rule has an automatic-cancellation framework unless shipment or express consent occurs within the specified period.

What happens if the new date slips too

A merchant that cannot meet its revised definite shipping date must seek consent to another delay before that date. The FTC's guidance makes an important distinction: silence cannot count as consent to a further extension in a renewed delay notice. If the required consent is not obtained and the goods have not shipped, the order must be canceled and a prompt refund provided under the rule.

Consider a hypothetical sneaker order with a promised October 10 shipment. Before October 10, the retailer provides a compliant notice moving shipment to October 25 and explains the cancellation option. If it later cannot ship by October 25, it cannot simply assume the buyer accepts a second extension. It must follow the renewed-notice rules. The example illustrates the process; it does not establish that any particular sneaker retailer missed its obligations.

The FTC defines prompt refunds in detail. Depending on the original payment method and the circumstances, the rule specifies the form and timing of a refund; for qualifying refunds it includes a seven-working-day standard. That does not mean every bank or card statement must display the credit instantly. Keep the cancellation confirmation and compare it with the seller's refund communication.

How to document a delayed sneaker order

Keep the product page or order confirmation showing the shipping promise, the checkout date, the payment record, every delay notice, and the carrier tracking history. Record the difference between an order that has been accepted, a shipping label that has been created, and a package actually handed to a carrier. The FTC's definition of shipment is about possession by the carrier, not merely generating a label.

When contacting customer support, ask for the promised ship-by date, the new expected shipping date, and the method for canceling and obtaining a refund. If the seller says the item is backordered without a firm new date, ask for the required delay options rather than assuming you must wait indefinitely.

This federal rule is not a general right to return shoes after delivery because the fit is wrong or because resale prices changed. Those questions depend on the applicable return policy and other law. Nor does it mean that every late delivery is a rule violation: the seller may have shipped on time even if a carrier delivered late. Focus first on the seller's shipping representation and the actual handoff to the carrier.

When the rule applies

The rule covers merchandise ordered online, by mail, or by telephone, regardless of the way it was advertised (16 CFR 435.1). It contains defined exclusions, so unusual transaction structures may need closer analysis, but ordinary prepaid online sneaker orders will generally be within its scope.

For shoppers, the practical sequence is straightforward: preserve the promised shipping date, check whether the merchandise actually shipped, read any delay notice for a revised date and cancellation option, and retain records of any refund request. The rule regulates how sellers handle shipping promises and delays; it does not guarantee that limited-release inventory will become available.
